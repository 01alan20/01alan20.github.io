# The Living University

> Narrated motion presentation. Numbers on the video must match the data package and source notes. Historical and projected material must be labeled. Seven chapters, 27 scenes. Five case-study scenes use the 2016–2025 official ANNUAL headcounts (10 data points per institution); do not synthesize missing observations.

## s01-opening {hold=0.8,transition=fade}
The story of higher education is changing. [#fields] What students complete, where potential learners live, and what a degree costs are all moving. [#question] Can universities adapt quickly enough?

## s02-majors {hold=0.8,transition=fade}
Start with the fields in which American universities award bachelor's degrees. [#race] Across the years, the rankings move as some disciplines expand faster than others. [#year] Each bar represents degrees awarded, not applications.

## s03-fields {hold=0.6,transition=fade}
A ranking only shows part of the story. [#share] Watch each field's share of the national total. [#lag] Completions tell us where students finished their studies, with a delay after their original choices.

## s04-portfolio {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
Universities have to respond to changing fields of study. [#portfolio] That affects program design, teaching capacity, career pathways, and decisions about which opportunities to develop.

## s05-geography {hold=0.8,transition=fade}
Now change the map. [#map] The number of high school graduates differs across states, and those differences will intensify. [#legend] Here we move from historical data to published projections.

## s06-demographics {hold=0.8,transition=fade}
The Western Interstate Commission for Higher Education projects a national peak in high school graduates in 2025. [#decline] By 2041, the total is projected to be about thirteen percent lower.

## s07-regions {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
But the national average hides regional differences. [#states] Most states are projected to have fewer graduates in 2041 than in 2023. [#response] Institutional strategy must reflect local conditions.

## s07a-three-institutions {hold=0.8,transition=fade}
Three universities followed different enrollment paths across ten years. [#three] We will follow Harvard, UNC Charlotte and Salem State, year by year, from 2016 through 2025. [#index] Each begins at an index of one hundred, so we can compare changes over time.

## s07b-harvard {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
Harvard's university-wide degree-student headcount changed unevenly. [#harvard] It grew through 2019, declined in 2020, rebounded sharply in 2021, and eased after 2022. [#context] By 2025, enrollment remained above its 2016 level.

## s07c-charlotte {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
UNC Charlotte expanded over the decade, with interruptions. [#charlotte] Enrollment rose to 2019, slipped in 2020, and fell again in 2022. Then it grew in each of the next three years, reaching 32,207 in 2025. [#pathways] That is a distinct public-university trajectory.

## s07d-salem {hold=0.9,transition=fade}
Salem State experienced a prolonged decline. [#salem] Its annual enrollment moved from 9,001 in 2016 to 6,009 in 2025, with decreases in every intervening year. [#response] The university has responded with access, affordability, recruitment and retention initiatives. Those actions should be evaluated on their own outcomes.

## s07e-perspective {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
Now watch all ten years for all three universities together. [#compare] Their enrollment trends diverge across the same period. The lines show what happened, but they do not establish why. [#question] To understand institutional effectiveness, we must also ask what happens after students arrive.

## s08-arrival {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
The three institutions serve different student markets. [#enter] Now move from institutional headcounts to the people behind them. [#cohort] Follow a national cohort after students start.

## s09-onehundred {hold=0.9,transition=fade}
Imagine one hundred students entering higher education. [#dots] Each dot represents one person in a national starting cohort. [#time] Move the clock forward six years.

## s10-outcomes {hold=1.5,transition=fade}
Of every hundred starters in the fall 2019 cohort, roughly sixty-one completed a credential within six years. [#complete] Nine were still enrolled. [#continuing] About thirty were no longer enrolled. [#stop] Every pathway has a different consequence.

## s11-risk {hold=0.6,transition=fade}
Real students encounter challenges at different times. [#friction] An unexpected bill, a course they cannot take, or support that arrives too late can disrupt progress. [#label] These examples illustrate possible friction points.

## s12-network {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
These challenges cross institutional boundaries. [#nodes] Admissions, finance, advising, registration, teaching, wellbeing, and careers all shape the learner experience. [#connections] Their connections deserve the same attention as their individual performance.

## s13-friction {hold=0.6,transition=fade}
Watch a student move through disconnected offices. [#break] Each handoff creates waiting time, repeated explanations, or missed information. [#signal] When information stays isolated, the next team may recognize the problem too late.

## s14-response {hold=0.8,transition=fade}
Now connect those offices. [#detect] An early signal reaches the right owner. [#act] The team coordinates an academic, financial, or wellbeing response. [#review] Progress is reviewed, and the plan changes when needed.

## s15-pillars {hold=0.8,transition=fade}
A strong learning system rests on five foundations. [#teach] High quality teaching. [#skills] Study skills. [#space] Spaces to learn. [#personal] Personal development. [#career] Professional development. Each foundation supports progress toward completion.

## s16-scenario {hold=0.8,transition=fade}
Run the student journey again, this time with clearer pathways and coordinated support. [#recover] Some illustrated risks are addressed earlier. [#caution] This is a design scenario, not a measured prediction of improved graduation rates.

## s17-cost {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
Now follow the financial side of that journey. [#ledger] Every additional term can bring tuition, living expenses, and time away from full time work. [#balance] The cost accumulates before a credential is earned.

## s18-paths {hold=0.7,transition=fade}
Three students can enter the same program and leave with very different outcomes. [#paths] One completes on time. Another takes longer. A third leaves without a credential. [#costs] Their costs and opportunities diverge.

## s19-earnings {hold=1.0,transition=fade}
National labor market data show a clear difference across educational attainment groups. [#bars] In 2025, median weekly earnings were higher for workers with bachelor's degrees than for workers with some college and no degree. [#note] These are group medians, not a forecast for any individual.

## s20-value {hold=1.0,transition=fade}
The economic question becomes more difficult when completion is uncertain. [#question] How much should someone invest in a degree, what support can they expect, and what happens if they cannot finish?

## s21-feedback {hold=0.9,transition=fade}
The answer requires a system that learns. [#loop] Demand informs academic programs. Student signals inform support. Completion and career outcomes inform the next decisions. [#adapt] Every cycle creates an opportunity to improve.

## s22-close {hold=2.4,transition=fade}
Twenty-first-century enrollment leadership designs the full learner relationship, from first interest through graduation, career outcomes, and alumni connection. [#final] The university works best when its systems work together.
