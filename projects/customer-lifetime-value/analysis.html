1. Clean the data and keep an audit trail¶

A sales line records one product on an invoice. We combine a customer’s positive sales lines on the same invoice into one completed order. Returns reduce revenue and never count as new orders.

5. Who keeps customers the longest?¶

The file can show how long customers keep buying during its two years. It cannot tell us when their relationship truly ends. For a fair country comparison, we look at customers with a complete 180 days and ask whether they still buy during days 150–179.

6. When should we expect a second purchase?¶

A second order is the first invoice recorded strictly after the first order’s timestamp. Same-timestamp invoices are excluded from this timing view. Every customer here has a complete 90-day follow-up; customers who never return still count in the total.

7. What is a customer worth after their first purchase?¶

Across identified customers, average observed net revenue is £2,842.69; the median is £862.75. These amounts cover each customer’s observed history, not a complete lifetime.

Separate the first sale from later customer value

For each eligible customer, we take net revenue during their first 180 days and subtract the original first basket’s sales. This prevents an expensive first order from automatically making the customer look valuable afterwards. Returns recorded during the window remain deducted, including refunds of the first order, so later net revenue can be negative.

Average later net revenue: £816.41 per customer.

The median is £213.53. Compare both: a small number of large buyers can raise the average substantially.

8. Does the first order tell us who will return?¶

We examine first-order spending, total items, product variety, specific products and pairs. Customers need a complete 180-day follow-up and at least one physical product in their first invoice. Item counts and product flags exclude service codes such as postage; basket spending includes all charges.

Group customers by what they spent on their first order. Then count how many placed another order within 90 days. The spending labels describe the first basket, not later spending and not only customers who returned.

11. What should the business do?¶

The first order gives us useful clues, but it cannot reliably tell us who will become a repeat customer.

First-order spending, quantity, product variety and product choice show useful patterns, but the later-customer prediction test remains limited. Adding product identities did not establish a clear improvement. Country and seasonal differences also affect how these patterns should be interpreted.

The second-order information improved the matched prediction test, providing a better basis for updating customers who have already returned. This does not resolve uncertainty about customers who have bought only once.

  • Use early signals to form groups for small retention tests, rather than treating them as certain customer outcomes.
  • Use the time-to-second-order evidence to choose reminder windows to test against a no-reminder group.
  • Judge customer value using later revenue as well as the first sale, and compare medians with averages.
  • Update the customer picture after a second purchase, while checking the matched-model evidence above.
  • Measure real campaign effects and product costs before making automatic spending decisions.

The analysis identifies where to investigate and what to test. A new time period and a randomized retention campaign would provide stronger evidence for deployment.
